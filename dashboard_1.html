The Buy-Timing Myth

A Claude-powered dashboard exploring a Gradient Boosting model built in Python, predicting Super Bowl secondary-market ticket prices.

The model built to find the best time to buy learned almost nothing about timing — instead, it learned who's playing.

Four findings below, in the order they unraveled.
Click any game across the charts below to trace it through every finding.
Finding 1 — the premise

Does buying earlier get you a better price?

Price moved in opposite directions before different Super Bowls — rising into kickoff for some, falling for others. There's no consistent pattern to learn — which is exactly why the tuned model ultimately gave zero weight to days-out-from-kickoff.

Finding 2 — why timing can't be the answer

The market doesn't even agree with itself

Super Bowl LIX, 3 days out: TickPick listed $6,552, StubHub listed $7,299 — a $747 gap for the identical game on the identical day. If platforms disagree this much on any given day, "buy on day X" was never going to hold up.

Finding 3 — what it learned instead

Spread barely matters. QB history matters a little.

Despite being the model's 2nd-most important feature (28%), how close the game was barely correlates with price on its own — r = -0.04, essentially no relationship.

Games with a QB who'd already played a Super Bowl in this window average 8% higher — real, but modest, given it's the model's single strongest feature (50%).
